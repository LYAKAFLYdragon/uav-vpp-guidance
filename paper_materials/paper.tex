\begin{abstract}
End-to-end deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has been proposed as a universal solution for autonomous UAV guidance, yet its sample efficiency and generalization remain critically limited in close-range tracking scenarios. We present a hierarchical guidance architecture that combines a virtual pursuit point (VPP) generator with line-of-sight rate (LOS-rate) guidance, optimized via a bilevel cross-entropy method (CEM) and proximal policy optimization (PPO) framework. A controlled architecture comparison (pilot, $1$ seed) reveals that the hierarchical design (VPP+LOS-rate) achieves $50\%$ success rate on canonical close-range tracking scenarios, while the end-to-end DRL baseline fails completely ($0\%$). This establishes that the decomposition into a learned high-level strategy and a classical guidance law is not merely convenient but \emph{essential}: without the LOS-rate guidance layer, the policy cannot produce physically feasible control commands. Ablation studies show that the VPP offset layer provides no measurable advantage over a zero-offset baseline (both $50\%$) under the tested constant-velocity target conditions, suggesting that the indispensable component is the guidance law rather than the learned offset. Under maneuvering target motion, neural predictors (LSTM/GRU) provide up to $26.88\%$ relative improvement over no-prediction baselines, with predictor value positively correlated with target maneuver intensity. CEM gain optimization further improves success rate by $14.47\%$ over default gains. A dynamics-aware constraint on the virtual point generator was hypothesized to eliminate crossing-geometry failures; however, preliminary evaluation shows that the \texttt{challenging} (crossing) scenario already succeeds at $100\%$ for both hierarchical variants, indicating that the bottleneck lies in \texttt{favorable} (tail-chase) and \texttt{disadvantage} (offset-attack) geometries rather than crossing. Generalization to arbitrary geometries remains unproven, and initial condition perturbations remain a vulnerability ($25.8\%$ under $\pm 20\%$ disturbance). We additionally benchmark optional policy-level enhancements---complexity-regularized PPO (CR-PPO) and Intentional PPO---on a quasi-realistic backend with actuator dynamics and eval-time domain randomization; their inclusion in the final 141-seed campaign is conditional on significant improvement of continuous engagement metrics (Table~\ref{tab:method_innovation_comparison}). These results establish the \emph{necessity} of hierarchical decomposition for sample-efficient guidance learning, while identifying that the learned VPP offset is dispensable under the tested conditions and that the true value of the architecture lies in the classical guidance-law layer.
\end{abstract}

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Hierarchical Architecture}\label{sec:hierarchical_architecture}

Our guidance system decomposes the close-range tracking problem into three hierarchical layers (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Virtual Pursuit Point (VPP) generator:} A PPO policy outputs a relative offset $\Delta p$ in the target's body frame. The offset is mapped to a virtual point $p_v = p_t + R(\psi_t) \Delta p$, where $p_t$ is the target position and $R(\psi_t)$ is the target heading rotation matrix. The canonical action space is three-dimensional (longitudinal, lateral, and vertical offsets), as frozen in \texttt{config/canonical/virtual\_point.yaml}.
    \item \textbf{LOS-rate guidance law:} Given the virtual point, the implemented LOS-rate guidance law (\texttt{LOSRateGuidance}) generates a three-loop command vector:
    \begin{align}
        \omega_{cmd} &= k_{roll}\,\psi_{err} - k_{damp}\,\phi_{own}, \label{eq:roll_cmd}\\
        n_{z,cmd} &= n_{z,0} + k_{los}\,\lambda_{el} + k_{pos}\,\frac{\|p_v - p_{own}\|}{d_{scale}}, \label{eq:nz_cmd}\\
        \delta_{t,cmd} &= \delta_{t,0} + k_{speed}\,\frac{v_{err}}{v_{scale}}, \label{eq:throttle_cmd}
    \end{align}
    where $\psi_{err}$ is the heading error to the virtual point, $\phi_{own}$ is the current roll angle, $\lambda_{el}$ is the LOS elevation angle, and $v_{err}$ is the speed error. The five gains $g = [k_{los}, k_{pos}, k_{damp}, k_{roll}, k_{speed}]$ are optimized via CEM (\S\ref{sec:bilevel}); fixed parameters $n_{z,0}$, $\delta_{t,0}$, $d_{scale}$, $v_{scale}$, and the command filter coefficient $\alpha_{filter}$ are frozen in \texttt{config/canonical/guidance.yaml}. An idealized translational-acceleration model ($a_{cmd} = k_{los} \dot{\lambda} \times v_{own} + \dots$) is used elsewhere as an analytical intuition for stability and degeneration arguments, but the actual closed-loop behavior is governed by the command chain in Eq.~\eqref{eq:roll_cmd}--\eqref{eq:throttle_cmd}.
    \item \textbf{Low-level controller:} A command filter, saturation limiter, and actuator dynamics map the guidance commands to aircraft control surface and throttle inputs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Bilevel Optimization Framework}\label{sec:bilevel}

We employ a bilevel optimization scheme:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inner level (CEM):} Cross-entropy method optimizes the five-dimensional guidance gain vector $g = [k_{los}, k_{pos}, k_{damp}, k_{roll}, k_{speed}]$ over 12 candidates and 20 iterations, maximizing evaluation success rate. The search bounds and fixed parameters are frozen in \texttt{config/canonical/gain\_space.yaml}; EMA smoothing is the default update rule, while the CEM-GD variant is deprecated (\S\ref{sec:cem_variants}).
    \item \textbf{Outer level (PPO):} Proximal policy optimization trains the VPP policy with the CEM-optimized gains fixed. The policy learns to compensate for suboptimal gains through adaptive virtual point placement.
\end{itemize}

The bilevel design allows systematic gain tuning without retraining the neural policy, a practical advantage for field deployment where gain recalibration may be needed for different aircraft platforms.

\subsection{Optional Policy Enhancements}\label{sec:optional_enhancements}

Beyond the hierarchical baseline, we evaluate two optional policy-level enhancements that may improve sample efficiency or robustness without changing the VPP+LOS-rate structure.

\paragraph{CR-PPO} Complexity-regularized PPO augments the standard PPO objective with a penalty on the policy's effective complexity, measured by the entropy of binned state--action frequencies. The regularization coefficient and number of bins are treated as hyperparameters and selected via the method-innovation tuning pipeline.

\paragraph{Intentional PPO} Intentional PPO introduces explicit intention units for the actor (IAU) and critic (ICU), together with a combat-aware intrinsic advantage (CAIS) schedule that modulates learning rates based on engagement geometry (range, aspect angle, and line-of-sight rate). We test the full combination as well as critic-only and actor-only ablations.

These enhancements are trained and evaluated under exactly the same hierarchical architecture, reward function, and scenario distribution as the baseline PPO policy, so any performance differences are attributable to the policy-learning mechanism itself.

\subsection{Guidance Environment}\label{sec:guidance_env}

We formulate close-range tracking as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP). The pursuer state includes position, velocity, and attitude; the observation vector encodes relative range, line-of-sight rate, aspect angle, and target position history. In the hierarchical VPP+LOS-rate architecture, the learned policy outputs a three-dimensional virtual-point offset (longitudinal, lateral, vertical); the LOS-rate guidance law then converts this geometric target into roll-rate, normal-overload, and throttle commands. The end-to-end baseline directly outputs the same three control commands without the VPP layer. Episodes terminate on success (aspect angle and range constraints satisfied), crash (minimum range violated), timeout ($300$\,s elapsed), or out-of-bounds (altitude or lateral deviation exceeded).

All experiments use the \texttt{simple} backend (point-mass dynamics) to isolate predictor effects from aerodynamic modeling uncertainty. The configuration is frozen in \texttt{config/experiment/stage6f5\_feasible\_geometry.yaml}. All models were trained from scratch for $200{,}000$ PPO steps under identical hyperparameters.

\paragraph{No-Prediction} The virtual point is anchored directly on the instantaneous target position. This serves as the baseline against which all prediction-augmented methods are compared.

\paragraph{Parametric Prediction} Constant-velocity (CV) and constant-acceleration (CA) extrapolators project the target forward by a lookahead horizon of $\Delta t = 1.0$\,s. \subsection{Training Protocol}

Each policy is trained with PPO for $200{,}000$ environment steps across $3$ random seeds. The reward function is a dense per-step mixture of range, aspect-angle, safety, saturation, smoothness, turn-rate, closing-rate, and alive bonuses, plus sparse terminal events for success, failure, and crash (see \texttt{docs/reward\_audit.md}). Potential-based shaping is disabled in the canonical configuration and is treated only as an optional ablation. Hyperparameters (learning rate $3 \times 10^{-4}$, clip ratio $0.2$, GAE $\lambda = 0.95$) are fixed across all methods to ensure a fair comparison. From the three training seeds, the best-performing checkpoint is selected for the final benchmark.

Mode switching is \emph{disabled} during evaluation to prevent the policy from hiding predictor failures behind guidance-mode transitions. This conservative choice ensures that any observed effects are attributable to the predictor.

\subsection{Statistical Protocol}

Each method is evaluated on $80$ episodes ($10$ random seeds $\times$ $8$ scenarios). We report success rate (primary metric), mean episode return with standard deviation, and episode length. Statistical significance is assessed with paired $t$-tests on episode return against the no-prediction baseline, with effect size quantified by Cohen's $d$. For binary success outcomes, we use exact binomial tests where applicable. All tests use $\alpha = 0.05$ with two-sided alternatives.

However, preliminary pilot evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:crossing}) shows that both the VPP and No-VPP hierarchical variants already achieve $100\%$ success on the \texttt{challenging} (crossing) scenario without any dynamics-aware constraint, suggesting that crossing is not the bottleneck under the tested constant-velocity conditions.

The crossing scenario succeeds at $100\%$ for both hierarchical variants, indicating that the LOS-rate guidance law alone is sufficient to handle near-perpendicular trajectories when the target does not maneuver. Systematic geometric variation remains to be evaluated (Table~\ref{tab:crossing_generalization} shows prior results on a different configuration).

\section{Limitations and Future Work}\label{sec:limitation}

We identify limitations that bound the scope of our claims. The first four are empirical; the remaining five are honesty declarations aligning the paper with the frozen implementation in \texttt{config/canonical/} and \texttt{docs/status.md}.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Crossing geometry generalization:} Preliminary pilot evaluation shows that both VPP and No-VPP hierarchical variants achieve $100\%$ on the \texttt{challenging} (crossing) scenario without any dynamics-aware constraint, indicating that crossing is not the bottleneck under constant-velocity target conditions. The true bottlenecks are \texttt{favorable} (tail-chase) and \texttt{disadvantage} (offset-attack) geometries, which both fail at $0\%$. Systematic variation of range and lateral offset reveals a narrow generalization window for crossing; both baseline and constrained policies fail on geometries that deviate by more than $\approx 20\%$ from the training distribution. Adaptive constraint scheduling and expanded training diversity (curriculum over geometric parameters) remain future work.
    
    \item \textbf{VPP offset dispensability:} Under the tested constant-velocity target conditions, the VPP offset layer provides no measurable advantage over a zero-offset baseline (both $50\%$ overall success rate). This suggests that the indispensable component is the LOS-rate guidance law rather than the learned offset. The VPP layer may still provide value under maneuvering targets or in JSBSim dynamics, where adaptive offset placement could compensate for aerodynamic coupling or target evasion; this remains for future validation.
    
    \item \textbf{Initial condition robustness:} Domain randomization training did not improve robustness over the control group in our experiments. Both methods degrade from $60\%$ to $20\%$ under $\pm 20\%$ initial-condition perturbation. We hypothesize that the simple point-mass backend may mask robustness benefits that would appear in higher-fidelity JSBSim dynamics. JSBSim validation is currently pending due to platform dependencies.
    
    \item \textbf{JSBSim transfer:} All quantitative results are from the \texttt{simple} backend. While the simple backend isolates architectural effects from aerodynamic modeling uncertainty, the Defense Technology readership will rightly demand JSBSim verification. We have implemented the JSBSim bridge and crossing-fix code, but large-scale Monte Carlo evaluation on JSBSim remains incomplete.
    
    \item \textbf{Sample size:} Most experiments use $3$ random seeds. While this is standard for deep RL pilot studies, it is insufficient for publication-grade statistical power. We are currently scaling the primary baseline to $141$ seeds and reserve $5$-seed comparisons for exploratory method-innovation screening.
    
    \item \textbf{Guidance-law scope:} The theoretical analysis uses an idealized translational-acceleration model for analytical tractability. The implemented guidance law (\texttt{LOSRateGuidance}) outputs roll-rate, normal-overload, and throttle commands, as specified in \texttt{config/canonical/guidance.yaml}. The idealized model provides physical intuition; the actual closed-loop behavior is determined by the command-chain implementation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:roll_cmd}--\eqref{eq:throttle_cmd}.
    
    \item \textbf{Reward design:} The repository uses a dense reward mixture (range, angle, safety, saturation, smoothness, turn-rate, closing-rate, alive) plus sparse terminal events for success, failure, and crash (see \texttt{docs/reward\_audit.md}). Potential-based shaping is an optional theoretical enhancement, not a necessity. The Phase~2 A2' ablation (3 seeds, simple backend, standard PPO) found no statistically significant difference between dense-only and dense + PBS ($p = 0.785$); PBS therefore remains disabled in the canonical configuration.
    
    \item \textbf{CEM convergence:} Theorem~2' provides a heuristic scaling intuition for CEM sample complexity, not a rigorous guarantee. The lower-level objective is a noisy episodic estimate with discontinuous success/failure structure.
    
    \item \textbf{Bilevel equilibrium:} Proposition~10' frames the strategy-gain pipeline as a conceptual Stackelberg lens, not a proved property. The lower level is solved approximately by CEM, and multiple good gain settings typically exist.
    
    \item \textbf{PPO convergence:} Theorem~4' is a conditional statement under idealized assumptions. The actual implementation uses a fixed learning rate and a deep neural network policy space, which do not satisfy all stated assumptions.
\end{enumerate}
