\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Hierarchical Decomposition is Effective---and Where It Is Not Enough}

The empirical advantage of the VPP+LOS-rate architecture over end-to-end DRL is not merely a hyperparameter difference: both architectures use identical PPO hyperparameters, observation spaces, and---in the JSBSim study---identical reward functions. The improvement stems from structural inductive bias. By fixing the guidance law to LOS-rate---a well-understood differential-geometric controller---the policy need only learn \emph{where} to place the virtual point, not \emph{how} to generate roll-rate, normal-overload, and throttle commands. This reduces the effective search space from the full command manifold to the lower-dimensional offset space, enabling faster convergence and better generalization.

The E2E baseline's persistent underperformance on neutral ($87.5\%$, $p = 5.0 \times 10^{-5}$) and challenging ($75\%$, $p = 4.8 \times 10^{-10}$) scenarios, even at $2$~M training steps, confirms that this gap is not merely a sample-efficiency issue---it is a structural limitation. The E2E policy's $12.5\%$ crash rate on both scenarios indicates that, even after exhaustive training, the policy fails to internalize the flight-envelope constraints that the LOS-rate guidance law provides by construction. In safety-critical guidance tasks, this structural reliability gap is a decisive argument for hierarchical decomposition.

\subsection{The VPP Safety Valve: Timeout as a Learned Survival Strategy}

The 5-seed JSBSim results on the disadvantage scenario reveal a pattern that reframes the VPP layer's contribution. VPP achieves $20\%$ success, with $20\%$ crash and $60\%$ timeout; No-VPP achieves $0\%$ success with $100\%$ crash. The key question is whether VPP's $60\%$ timeout rate represents a failure of the architecture or a rational response to the reward structure.

The reward function imposes a crash penalty of $-600$ and a timeout penalty of $-300$. A policy that faces a binary choice between (a) an aggressive lead-turn maneuver with high crash probability and (b) a conservative stand-off strategy that exhausts the time budget should rationally prefer (b) when the crash probability exceeds $50\%$. The VPP policy's mean offset of 2987~m on disadvantage---$3.2\times$ larger than on the next-hardest scenario---is direct behavioral evidence that the policy has learned this trade-off. By placing the virtual point far ahead of the target, the policy commands a shallower intercept trajectory that preserves altitude and avoids the high-descent-rate maneuver that causes No-VPP to crash.

This interpretation is consistent with the reward-weighted training objective: the policy optimizes expected return, not success rate per se. The timeout outcome is therefore not a failure of the architecture but a correct optimization of the specified objective under high uncertainty. Converting timeout into success requires either (a) changing the reward weights to tolerate more crash risk, or (b) curriculum learning that progressively tightens the acceptable range envelope while the policy learns to navigate the disadvantage geometry safely. We consider (b) the more promising direction, as (a) would likely increase the crash rate toward the No-VPP baseline.

\subsection{VPP Offset is Not a Performance Booster in Canonical Geometries---And That Is Expected}

The simple-backend comparison shows comparable VPP and No-VPP success rates ($70.0\%$ vs $73.3\%$). On JSBSim, the two methods are identical on three of four scenarios. This pattern is consistent with the architecture's design intent: the LOS-rate guidance law, with CEM-optimized gains, already generates adequate lead-turn and collision-avoidance behavior in nominal geometries. The VPP offset adds degrees of freedom that are only needed when the guidance law alone is insufficient---specifically, in the disadvantage geometry where the target's initial advantage and maneuvering dynamics create a flight-envelope bottleneck.

This finding has an important practical implication: for deployment scenarios where disadvantage engagements can be avoided by operational constraints (e.g., rules of engagement that prevent engaging from a position of tactical inferiority), the simpler zero-offset variant is sufficient. The VPP layer is a safety retrofit for the worst-case geometry, not a universal performance enhancer.

\subsection{CEM-EMA Gain Optimization: Performance Driver, but Architecture-Conditional}

CEM-EMA tuning improves success rate from $60.2\%$ to $74.7\%$ ($+14.47$ pp) on the simple backend, confirming it as a major performance driver. However, the JSBSim results qualify this finding: even with CEM-optimized gains, the No-VPP variant crashes at $100\%$ on disadvantage. The VPP offset and CEM-optimized gains are therefore complementary rather than competing: gains provide performance in nominal conditions, while the offset provides safety margin in adverse conditions. The architecture's value lies in combining both, not in choosing between them.

From a deployment perspective, the hierarchical architecture offers two practical advantages: (1) the guidance law can be certified or analyzed independently of the learned policy, and (2) the CEM-EMA gain optimizer can be re-tuned for different aircraft platforms without retraining the neural policy. The zero-offset variant is the simplest certified configuration; the VPP variant adds a learned safety margin for worst-case geometries without changing the certifiable guidance-law core.

\subsection{Statistical Power: Episode-Level vs Seed-Level Tests}

The disadvantage comparison between VPP and No-VPP illustrates an important methodological point. The seed-level paired $t$-test yields $p = 0.374$ (non-significant), while the episode-level Fisher exact test yields $p = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}$ (highly significant). This discrepancy arises from the seed-level variance structure: VPP seed~0 achieves $100\%$ on disadvantage while seeds~1--4 achieve $0\%$, creating a within-group standard deviation of $44.7$~pp that overwhelms the between-group difference of $20$~pp. In contrast, the No-VPP seeds all exhibit $0\%$ success (zero variance).

The Fisher test correctly pools all 200 episodes, detecting the $20/100$ vs $0/100$ success pattern with high precision. We report both tests transparently and recommend that future multi-seed RL evaluations pre-register the analysis level (seed vs episode) to avoid post-hoc test selection. For the present study, the directional evidence from both tests is consistent: VPP outperforms No-VPP on disadvantage, and the episode-level Fisher test provides the appropriate statistical resolution for this comparison.
